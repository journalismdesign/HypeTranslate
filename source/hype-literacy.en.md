This course introduces the study of hype as an urgent force that shapes technological development, economics, politics and journalism. It is suited for people working in journalism and media but also serves as an introduction to hype for anybody interested, such as students, designers, academics or writers.

News circulating around new emerging technologies like AI, Quantum or new space travel promise radical transformation. Problematically, these narratives repeat industry promises and apocalyptic visions instead of questioning them. These days, for example, headlines promise that “AI will change everything:” AI is framed as a "revolutionary force", a "deadly threat" or even treated like a god-like entity. Much of the current media landscape has become an uncritical amplifier of hype - yet, hype is not neutral.

The course modules explain how hype shapes public perception, generates attention, wealth, and innovation while simultaneously distorting priorities, silencing alternatives, and deepening global inequalities.

This is why journalists need hype literacy. In this in-depth self-paced course, journalists will learn how to recognize these dynamics, to unmask the playbook of hype, and to report on AI with nuance.

The courses was created by authors stemming from the [hypestudies.org](https://hypestudies.org/) ecosystem. ## Hype: What is it?

Hype is characterized by the fascination with the future: Exaggerated and unrealistic promises of value development are coupled with a strident optimism that captures the attention of investors, technologists, politicians, journalists, and the general citizenry. 
 
Although usually regarded as a natural process, hype is never accidental: It is strategically crafted by tech and PR companies and their CEOs to over-stress the positive implications of technology while undermining the negative ones.

Media and journalism play a vital role in this process. They amplify hype and let it circulate through society. As an example, look at recent dramatic headlines about AI from major news outlet covers:

```image
assets/cours/bildschirmfoto-2025-12-17-um-10-29-34.jpg :: 2025 front covers of major news publications like the Daily Mail, DER SPIEGEL, TIME, and The Times.
```

Looking at these headlines we can see that hype leverages uncertainty and emotions like hopes and fears, linking these to strongexpectations. The wording and cover images convert AI into a menacing threat that can dominate humanity - or promise to liberate humanity from tedious work.

These strong claims call for attention. They highlight the transformative power of AI and thereby legitimize certain standpoints and scenarios, or platform certain figures and their alleged expertise.

Journalists come across the issue of hype when they craft articles or evaluate the news value of certain events competing for clicks. Economic pressures through search engines and social media platforms often lead their work in framing, headlining, and choosing topics with the goal of attracting as many readers as possible.

For a long time, communication studies has traced the fluctuating waves of media attention by looking at the world of news, social media, or marketing. The peak of such attention is also understood as “sensationalism” or “frenzy.” Hype is very much connected to these phenomena of increased media attention, but integrates many more actors and aspects as introduced below.

### It takes a society to hype

Hype is a dynamic that rests on the *circulation* of promotion. This means that one cannot hype alone. There are actors that trigger and lead hype, and actors that amplify and let hype circulate throughout society.

When you think about the expectations and claims made around a new technology, for example, one observes that there are actors like:

| **VISIBLE ACTOR** | **ROLE** |
|---|---|
| **LEADERS **(Producers of hype) |  |
| **Big Tech companies** | Create technological visions, control infrastructures, orchestrate narratives. |
| **Startups & founders** | Promote radical futures to attract visibility, investment, and partnerships. |
| **Corporate research labs** | Provide scientific legitimacy and authoritative forecasts. |
| **Scientists & engineers** | Serve as epistemic authorities whose statements are often taken as fact. |

## Hype's emotional economy: *It's all about the show!*

As already clarified above, a hyper alone cannot cause a hype: It needs an audience to rise and be carried. This audience is not just a passive spectator, but a complicit collaborator acting like a catalyst. As scientist Jack Stilgoe writes: *“Technological hype is not just exaggeration, nor is it idle speculation; it is an act of persuasion.”*

The repertoire of impression management in hypes involves figurative language that can trigger emotions to craft a community. This can take the form of bold statements and exaggerated claims that hail future benefits, involving fabulous potentials and shiny prospects that need to be mobilized. Hypes often maneuver within the binaries of hopes vs. fears, opportunity vs. a closing window, or redemption vs. doom. These elements are parts of storytelling, like fairy tales that need suspense. Problematically, they also strongly simplify reality.

Unlike economic bubbles, hypes refer to a social dynamic that has an identity-forming component. A hype describes a moment of emotional ecstasy that unites people behind it in a "followership." The promising invitation to conquer the seemingly unattainable, innocent or unprecedented triggers feelings of belonging. Such dynamics point to the psychological appeal that hype can exert. Followers feel part of a special mission, assuming that they share an insight that others do not see. One is part of a selected group of people who “know better” and have “insights.”

Also, hype reacts to the complexity of the everyday life experience, which can be stressful and disorienting. There lies a deep wish within hypes to escape (even only for a moment) everything that entangles and complicates the innocent declaration of a bold promise or a golden future on the horizon. So, in a nutshell: Hype is a show and uses theatrical elements of play, stage impression or story telling to take an audience on a journey.

## Hypes are not about truths or lies - but getting away with it!

Good reporting depends on checking facts and confirming what’s actually happening before sharing it with the public. With hype, this practice is often turned upside-down.

Hypers are masters in coining uncertainty and vagueness about the future into an overly optimistic story of opportunity, thereby possibly misleading decision - makers or overshadowing alternative futures. It is not that hypers lie - it is rather that they do not care about categories of truth or lie, about the difference between fact and belief. Hypes exploit the uncertainty of the future instead of giving orientation through structuring and curating it, as for example, scenarios do. Wild and bold hypotheses about, for instance, accelerating growth, vast sector transformations through quantum computing or machines gaining consciousness are just some of the many examples.

Hypes are more like what philosopher Frankfurt discusses with the concept of “bullshit.” He conceptualizes the relation between truth and bullshit in the following manner:

```citation
auteur: Harry Frankfurt, 2005, p. 55
portrait: assets/cours/harry-frankfurt-at-2017-acls-annual-meeting-1.jpg

*The fact about himself that the bullshitter hides [...] is that the truth-values of his statements are of no central interest to him […] This does not mean that his speech is anarchically impulsive, but that the motive guiding and controlling it is unconcerned with how the things about which he speaks truly are.*
```

Not all hypers are bullshitters - but hypers have an ambivalent relationship to the truth. As hype is about persuasion, promotion and improvisation, it uses color and imaginative play. The bolder a technological promise and the greater the growth and investment opportunities sketched on the horizon, the further we distance ourselves from the truth but enter highly speculative territory.

A great example of how hype has little to do with truth and validity, but more with bullshit, are Tesla product releases. Check out the video where Elon Muks hails the indestructible armour glass of the cybertuck - which goes terribly wrong.

## Conclusion — Chapter 1

Many of the aspects raised below will be discussed further in the coming chapters, so don't worry if not all aspects are completely clear to you yet. This is just to give you a comprehensive overview and awareness of the different facets of hype. Why hype is so strongly linked to technology, how it can be really harmful for society, and how news coverage can fall into its trap - and avoid it - will be part of the next chapters.

The best way to understand hype is that it does things:

1. Makes uncertain things look certain 
 Turns guesses about the future into “inevitable” outcomes.
2. Speeds everything up 
 Creates urgency and pressure so people act fast without fully thinking things through.
3. Bends the facts 
 Leads to exaggerated claims, shaky predictions, and low-quality evidence.
4. Plays with emotions 
 Generates excitement and hope in the beginning, and later frustration or fear when promises fail.
5. Sells stories, not reality 
 Relies on big promises, catchy buzzwords, and selective framing to grab attention.
6. Moves money and resources 
 Pushes funding, talent, and political attention toward whatever is being hyped.
7. Aligns people around the same story 
 Gets different actors - companies, media, policymakers - to repeat the same talking points.
8. Silences critique 
 Presents one possible future as the only sensible or desirable one.
9. Locks people into early commitments 
 Encourages governments and organisations to adopt tools or build infrastructure before knowing if they work.
10. Lets private companies set the rules 
 Allows firms to shape public debate and policy as if they were neutral experts.
11. Concentrates power 
 Benefits the companies and investors who control the narrative, sidelining everyone else.
12. Skews political agendas 
 Pushes policymakers to prioritise what the hype industry wants, not what evidence suggests.
13. Fuels booms and busts 
 Creates market frenzies followed by sudden crashes.
14. Helps to make the future it sells 
 By repeating the same narrative everywhere, hype can actually bring parts of that future into reality.

# Chapter 2: Hype in promissory technologies

## Overview

Technology coverage often treats innovation as inevitable progress, but hype operates through deeper cultural and political mechanisms. Journalists must understand how and why promissory narratives around new technology shape public imagination and transform speculation into obligation. This chapter equips you with frameworks to recognize technological enchantment, decode promissory rhetoric, and question whose futures are being sold. By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- understand how promises create social momentum around uncertain technologies.
- recognize patterns of technological enchantment and leapfrogging narratives.
- identify the promise–requirement cycle that turns speculation into obligation.

## Why hype and technology?

So far we implicitly took one thing for granted: Hype and technology are connected. But, can there be a hype around a vacuum cleaner the same way as there is a hype around AI?

The answer: It depends how much transformational potential is projected onto a technology. Because this is what technology is about - societal change. Hype works so well here because the phenomenon taps into a familiar cultural story we hear every day, namely, the belief that new technologies naturally bring progress and make old truths and established ways of being obsolete.

## Hype and modernity go together

One is inevitably drawn to capitalism and its mantra of efficiency, new markets and social transformation when looking at hype in this context.

But hype also exists and existed in other forms of societies. In the 20th century, communist regimes in the Soviet Union crafted the figure of the Soviet Bolshevik as the “new man,” leading the “avantgarde.” Dictators like Mao or Stalin caged humans inside a discourse of modernization.They stigmatized a whole class (the proletariat) with the constant necessity of change and state-led top-down social engineering.

## Technological enchantment and lock-in

When people encounter the marvels of technological transformation, many are carried away by an enthusiastic image of a future that has yet to unfold. What hype then does is to amplify these promises. It makes them louder, more urgent, and harder to question, highlighting potential benefits while leaving risks and externalities out of view. Societies are blinded by technological enchantment.

```note
Technological enchantment can be described as a surprising encounter, a meeting with something that you did not expect and are not fully prepared to engage with. Itrefers to the sense of wonder, fascination, or even magical allure that people experience when interacting with advanced or innovative technologies. The term blends the idea of "technology" — the practical application of scientific knowledge — with "enchantment" — a feeling of delight, mystery, or being spellbound.
```

This enchantment also has political consequences. By presenting innovation as unstoppable and inherently positive, hype obscures the power dynamics behind technological development. It can frame lobbying as neutral expertise or make corporate interest look like public interest. Let's stick to space travel as an example. When NASA was in crisis in 2010, it was the Obama government that enabled the privatization of space travel, [financing Elon Musk's spaceX with public money](https://fee.org/articles/does-obama-deserve-credit-for-elon-musk-s-spacex-triumph-yes-and-no/). The commercialization of space goes hand in hand with the defending of global American clout and the securitization of new markets. This creates very big private-public dependencies. Today, Elon Musk can herald himself as a figure who defends public American interest, from Starlink constellations, to SpaceX.

A special version of technological enchantment is Leapfrogging. Leapfrogging is the promissory idea that countries in the Global South can skip slower stages of development by adopting the newest technologies. In practice, leapfrogging rarely happens: Advanced technologies still depend on reliable infrastructures, stable institutions, and local capacity that cannot simply be 'jumped over.'

As Rose M. Mutiso, the director of the [African Tech Futures Lab](https://www.africantechfutures.org/) (2025) [writes](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adz9028):

Understanding how promises and enchantment work helps journalists to distinguish between aspiration and evidence, revealing whose futures are being promoted and whose realities are left out of the picture.

The problem is that as promises of technological progress and leapfrogging circulate, they quickly become expectations and then requirements. Institutions, companies or states can easily feel obliged to invest in or adopt a technology simply because the promised future has gained social momentum - even when evidence is limited (also known as *lock-in *effect).

Journalists have a special role here, as they can increase or decrease pressure on public institutions and politicians by amplifying uncritically blueprint visions from tech companies or iluminating the public about the costs and risks behind shiny prospects.

Hype accelerates this process by, as discussed in the previous chapter, adding urgency and inevitability, and turning speculative visions into obligations that shape decisions long before the technology is proven.

# Chapter 3: The politics of hype

## Overview

Hype is never neutral: It concentrates power, shapes agendas, and marginalizes alternative futures. Understanding hype as a political force means recognizing how it functions as governance, determines whose voices matter, and what role it plays in an economy based on risks and debts.

This chapter provides frameworks to analyze the power dynamics behind technological narratives and identify whose interests are being served.

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- recognise hype as a mechanism of power and governance
- identify which actors produce, amplify, and benefit from hype
- question whose futures are made visible and whose remain hidden

```section
1
```

## Why hype is a matter of power

Hype is a form of power because it helps define what the future should look like, who will shape it, and how we’ll get there. It doesn’t just communicate ideas; it directs attention, sets priorities, and limits what people think is possible. In doing so, it favours the interests and worldviews of those who already hold structural advantage: Corporations, governments, elite research institutions, and well-connected experts.

Hype also operates as a form of anticipation. By projecting confident promises or warnings about the future, powerful actors can steer present action by mobilising resources, influencing regulation, and attracting political support long before technologies actually work.

### Hype as an instrument for agenda setting

In practice, hype acts as an agenda-setting instrument: It determines which technological issues become urgent, which stories dominate public debate, and which concerns are sidelined. When a technology is hyped, it becomes difficult for journalists, policymakers, and institutions to ignore it — regardless of its actual maturity or evidence base.

As a result, public and institutional agendas often shift around hype. Resources flow toward what is framed as “the next big thing,” and away from sectors that lack similar narrative force.

```note
The recent dominance of AI in public discourse is a clear example: Both in Europe and the United States, recent years have seen massive public and private investment flowing toward AI, while climate funding, though substantial, often struggles to maintain political momentum despite increasingly urgent scientific warnings.
```

What gets framed as urgent, transformative, or economically promising shapes where talent, capital, and political will flow. Often, with genuine consequences for what problems get solved and which languish.

### Hype as a mechanism of governance

Hype not only shapes public conversation; it also governs how institutions make decisions. When powerful actors present technological futures as both desirable and inevitable, they push governments, regulators, and public institutions into a reactive mode — one shaped more by anticipation and fear of falling behind than by evidence or democratic deliberation.

Big Tech companies and elite research labs use hype to position themselves as the natural stewards of technological futures. Through crisis narratives, technical expertise, and promises of safety, these actors claim privileged authority over what needs to be built, protected, or regulated.

```note
A clear example of this dynamic can be seen in debates around Artificial General Intelligence (AGI). Prominent companies and figures influenced by longtermist thinking often claim that AGI poses unprecedented existential risks – risks that are themselves speculative because they might happen in the future. On this basis, they argue that decisions about how to build or regulate these systems should be made primarily by technical experts inside a small group of specialised labs. Public oversight, democratic deliberation, and broader societal participation are framed as too slow, too uninformed, or too dangerous given the “stakes.” As a result, speculative futures are used to justify concentrating power in private institutions—effectively shifting decisions about the collective future from democratic spaces to technical elites.
```

Hype also legitimises anticipatory governance: Policies justified not by present realities but by speculative risks and imagined breakthroughs, like those presented by the existential risk narratives. In this situation, expectations about uncertain futures can be mobilised to redirect resources, reshape political priorities, or justify exceptional measures. These expectations become political instruments.

In this way, hype reconfigures state agendas around technological imperatives. Instead of governing technologies in the public interest, governments increasingly govern toward them, aligning policies, investments, and public narratives with the visions promoted by the most influential hypemakers. The result is a shift in power: democratic oversight weakens while private actors gain disproportionate influence over the direction of technological and societal development.

```note
The venture capitalist Peter Thiel has [stated](https://slate.com/news-and-politics/2012/02/investor-peter-thiel-is-the-billionaire-behind-ron-pauls-presidential-campaign.html) that “one of the things I like about technology is that when technology’s unregulated you can change the world without getting approval from other people. At its best, it’s not subject to democratic control, and not subject to the majority, which I think is often hostile to change.”
```

### Hype as a threat to democracy

Democratic decision-making requires deliberation, time, and the inclusion of diverse perspectives. Hype works in the opposite direction: It compresses time by creating a sense of urgency that discourages scrutiny and frames democratic processes as barriers to progress.

This acceleration legitimizes private actors as the de facto governors of key technological infrastructures, sidelining public oversight. Overpromissory narratives displace evidence-based policymaking, while media hype narrows debate and weakens epistemic pluralism by amplifying only a small set of voices and visions. These dynamics enable agenda capture, policy distortion, and public disempowerment, allowing speculative futures to shape political priorities without meaningful democratic control.

### Legitimacy

For a technological promise to shape public debate, attract resources, or influence policy, it must be perceived as legitimate. Expectations become powerful only when they are authorised by institutions, expert communities, and cultural narratives that frame them as credible and desirable. Without legitimacy, hype remains noise; with it, hype becomes a political force.

Legitimacy is, however, profoundly uneven. Only certain actors possess the structural authority required to define plausible futures: Big Tech companies, elite research labs, influential think tanks, well-connected governments, and experts who already occupy privileged epistemic positions. Because these actors are embedded in institutions that confer trust, their statements about the future — whether cautious or exaggerated, utopian or catastrophic — travel far. They can mobilise urgency, shape agendas, and influence decisions long before evidence exists.

Journalists play an essential role in this process. Due to sourcing routines, time pressure, and the need for reliable expert commentary, newsrooms tend to amplify voices that already come with institutional legitimacy. As a result, the same actors repeatedly appear as authoritative interpreters of technological futures, reinforcing the hierarchy of who is allowed to speculate and whose perspectives are excluded.

This concentration of legitimacy has political consequences. Actors with high legitimacy can circulate sweeping promises or warnings about the future, often with little immediate scrutiny. Long timelines, dramatic claims, and ambitious visions are more easily accepted when they come from trusted institutions. This dynamic grants powerful actors the ability to suspend critique, create urgency, and frame the future according to their own interests.

```note
In practice, legitimacy determines whose futures count. It shapes who can make claims about technological development, who is believed, and whose visions are taken seriously in the public arena.
```

### Uncertainty

The tech industry is always projecting possible futures, but hype does not resolve the uncertainty that surrounds them — it organizes it. By projecting confidence and inevitability, hype creates the illusion of certainty while relying on vagueness, ambiguity, and long timelines that shield actors from accountability.

In high-tech narratives, uncertainty becomes productive: Indeterminate futures allow powerful actors to imagine bold scenarios and mobilise resources toward them. In this way, uncertainty is made actionable — the less clear and predictable the future is, the easier it becomes to justify investment, delay scrutiny, and dismiss critique — turning uncertainty itself into a source of power.

Newsrooms and media platforms can become part of this dynamic when hype drives attention, visibility, or professional authority. Each of these actors participates in shaping narratives that favour their own interests, resources, and institutional agendas.

```note
"There is absolutely no inevitability as long as there is a willingness to contemplate what is happening,” said media scholar Marshall McLuhan. This shows that the goal of hype is not to convey knowledge about the future, but solely to generate attention, momentum, and accumulate capital.
```

Hype is woven into our economic and institutional structures. Beyond driving investment flows, attracting partnerships, and fueling the public, it also deepens institutional dependency on Big Tech infrastructures — from cloud services to data tools.

This also reinforces colonial dynamics, where hype leaders and followers profit from invisibilized actors while exploiting them and their resources at a labour, environmental and energetic level.

Since hype creates frameworks of desirable futures and heroic figures, it works as an engine of cultural transformation, reinforcing cultural subjects like the “early adopter,” the "entrepreneur" or the “crypto-bro.”

## Conclusion - Chapter 3

The motivations behind hype are strategic. For some actors, hype is a pathway to capital and market advantage. For others, it provides geopolitical positioning, framing national futures around technological leadership. Many rely on hype for public legitimacy, using ambitious visions to appear innovative or future-oriented. Others pursue institutional funding by presenting emerging technologies as essential to economic growth or national security. Across this ecosystem, hype functions as a tool for securing relevance, influence, and resources in contexts of uncertainty — making bold futures not just communicative choices, but political and economic strategies.

Understanding these motivations and realizing who produces, who amplifies and what gets hidden in hype helps journalists recognize that hype is not simply enthusiasm — hype is a matter and maker of power.

Journalism doesn't exist in a vacuum — it operates within systems that can inadvertently amplify hype rather than scrutinize it. ## ## The nature of news production

### Time pressures and the 24/7 news cycle

The always-on news environment means we're often working with incomplete information. Early sources — whether they're company spokespeople, researchers seeking attention, or officials managing a crisis — frequently have strong incentives to shape the narrative before the full picture emerges. Even with the best intentions, the first published version of a story is rarely the most complete.

```note
Consider the coverage of OpenAI's GPT-4 launch in March 2023. Initial reports, driven by carefully managed PR materials and embargoed briefings, emphasized capabilities and potential applications. The race to cover the story meant many outlets published pieces emphasizing dramatic use cases and revolutionary potential before experts could contextualize the technology's limitations, existing similar systems, or the serious questions about training data and accuracy.
```

### ## Incentives for novelty, conflict and simplicity

Stories framed as breakthroughs or dramatic shifts simply perform better, meaning they generate interest and reach. But this creates predictable distortions:

- Complex, incremental developments get repackaged as sudden revolutions
- Nuanced findings get flattened into binary claims (“good” vs. “bad”, “healthy” vs. “unhealthy”, “innovative” vs. “outdated”)
- The most dramatic elements overshadow more representative but quieter facts

```note
Media coverage of Theranos illustrates this perfectly. The founder Elizabeth Holmes' narrative of revolutionizing blood testing with a single drop of blood was inherently dramatic — a young Stanford dropout taking on an entire industry. Major outlets amplified this framing, with covers and profiles celebrating the “breakthrough” before technical journalists like John Carreyrou at the Wall Street Journal [could investigate whether the technology actually worked](https://www.wsj.com/articles/theranos-has-struggled-with-blood-tests-1444881901). The novelty angle made early scepticism seem like missing the story.
```

### The fear of missing out

Hype gains its real momentum by pointing to vast opportunities that lie ahead, which require the right timing if great potential is not to be lost. Hence, temporality is a crucial dimension for understanding and negotiating technology expectations. Popular technology narratives often play with history to elicit grandeur, just to tell followers and investors that now is the right moment to invest.

To produce maximum traction and a feeling of urgency, hype can be described through the ancient Greek word *Kairos*, meaning 'the right or critical moment,' also known as the ‘closing window of opportunity.’ Actors will frame emerging technologies as mirror images of past ‘revolutions,’ or unbelievable transformations.

“Invest now!” - before it is too late to be part of something big. “Act now and become an early adopter!” - before others are quicker than you and seize the fortune! A missed chance, or a bleak future if not acted on now, is a common rhetorical motive to spur action. Thus, stressing the opportunity costs is a distinctive feature of hypers, who urge followers to act instantly, take risks and think boldly. In hype, the future is to be conquered!

The anxiety about being “late to the conversation” can also push newsrooms to publish preliminary takes. Once a story gains momentum, waiting for verification can feel like a professional risk.


### Shrinking budgets: Doing more with less

This is the uncomfortable truth we all live with: Newsrooms are forced to do more with less. The result is that fact-checking often becomes reactive, catching errors after publication, rather than proactive verification before publication.

This “[churnalism](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/21670811.2017.1355026)” — the practice of republishing press releases with minimal additional reporting — is most visible in technology and health coverage. When a pharmaceutical company announces early promising trial results, many outlets will publish stories based primarily on the company's press materials, sometimes within hours, without waiting for independent expert review or accessing the underlying data. This has happened frequently with oncology drugs, Alzheimer’s treatments, and weight-loss medications.

## The role of PR, lobbying, and research hype

### How to manufacture “news”

As noted in chapter 3, hype has the power to set agendas, and professional communicators understand journalism's vulnerabilities and design materials accordingly:

- Press releases emphasize strong claims and simple messaging
- Embargoed reports and exclusive briefings make journalists feel they have special access
- Public demos present prototypes as if they're market-ready solutions

```note
The announcement of “flying cars” or “hyperloop” systems often follows this pattern: Dramatic unveiling, impressive concept footage, claims about timelines just far enough away (5-10 years) to avoid immediate verification, with companies often referencing each other, and follow-up coverage that fades before delivery dates arrive. Each new company in the space generates fresh hype cycles despite decades of unfulfilled similar promises.
```

Hype often generates amplification spirals, where early coverage triggers more attention, which in turn justifies further reporting, creating a self-reinforcing cycle. Once the cycle begins, hyped technologies rapidly become cultural and political priorities. This dynamic does not simply reflect technological reality; it actively constructs it.

This dynamic is made worse by the fact that critiques of such concepts seldom have the resources of the companies selling them. While the positive stories about coming disruptions and transformation reach newsrooms nicely packaged, important counter-arguments are something that journalists have to actively search for.

```note
The “metaverse” hype cycle of 2021-2022 demonstrates this perfectly. When Facebook rebranded to Meta, the company's massive investment and bold claims about the future of online interaction generated thousands of stories. Most coverage adopted Meta’s framing about an imminent shift in how we'd work, socialize, and play, with a large number of other startups and companies also referencing the idea publicly. By 2023, when adoption remained minimal and Meta’s own executives downplayed the timeline, the story had largely faded from coverage rather than generating proportional scrutiny about why the predictions didn’t materialize. In March 2026. Meta signaled a significant retreat from its metaverse ambitions, pulling back from its VR social platform, after its VR subsidiary company [lost $19.2 billion](https://investor.atmeta.com/investor-news/press-release-details/2026/Meta-Reports-Fourth-Quarter-and-Full-Year-2025-Results/default.aspx#:~:text=The%20second%2Dlargest%20contributor%20to,to%20be%2013%2D16%25.) in 2025.
```

In this way, journalists become central actors in this process. Even when trying to be critical, journalists operate within hype-driven environments that privilege novelty, speed, and pre-existing legitimacy. Their coverage helps define what societies talk about — and, crucially, what they neglect.

### Miscommunication of scientific uncertainty

Science generally trades in probabilities, confidence intervals, and carefully hedged conclusions. News, in contrast, often tends toward more binary framings:

- “Study shows X works” vs. “Study finds weak correlation between X and Y in a specific population”
- “Scientists warn of catastrophic risk” vs. “Models suggest range of scenarios depending on policy choices”

Importantly, limitations sections of papers, which researchers include for good reason, often disappear in press releases

```note
Nutritional science coverage epitomizes this problem. Studies showing associations between diet and health outcomes (often in specific populations, with limited effect sizes) get translated into simple advice: “Coffee prevents cancer” or “Wine makes you live longer.” When later studies show contradictory results, the public sees confusion rather than the normal scientific process of refining understanding through accumulated evidence.
```

- Institutional incentives matter: When sources have something to gain, extra scepticism is warranted

# Chapter 5: How to cover hype

## Overview

Technology reporting is often shaped as much by persuasion as by facts. Journalists must navigate claims that exaggerate impact, compress uncertainty, or promise inevitable change. This chapter equips you with practical tools to spot hype, verify speculative claims, and provide context.

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- identify rhetorical traps in tech coverage
- go beyond hype in your reporting
- ask better questions in the process

```section
1
```

## ### Rhetorical traps

Despite technology’s aura of cold rationality, hype is *explicitly emotional*. The stories told must resonate with people to be believed and acted upon. To accomplish this, hype relies on a number of rhetorical tricks and tools.

```tri
Buzzwords :: 5G | Metaverse | Artificial Intelligence | Future of work
False Urgency & FOMO :: “act now” | “before it’s too late” | “don't risk being left out”
Grandiose predictions :: “revolutionary” | “game-changing” | “disruptive” | “paradigm shift”
Valuations & investment Hype :: “unicorn startup” | “billion-dollar market” | “explosive growth”
```

### How to counteract these rhetorical traps

```accordeon
+ Buzzwords
Buzzwords are shorthands for complex technological fields, simplifying them with a catchy name. They may develop from terms with specific meanings in specialist jargon and become fashionable among non-specialists, losing their precise meaning in the process.

Buzzwords serve a promotional function: They compress uncertainty into a confident label that implies progress and inevitability. Their vagueness allows many actors (companies, investors, policymakers) to project their own hopes or interests onto the same word. This elasticity makes them powerful tools for persuasion and hype.

Questions to ask:

- What does the speaker mean by this term in concrete, operational terms? If in doubt, ask the speaker to clarify.
- Does the term mean the same thing in policy papers, academic research, and press releases? If not, what might explain those differences?

+ False urgency & FOMO
The timeframe for adopting or reacting to a technological novelty is explicitly presented as short, with time quickly running out. This framing positions hesitation as risk and compliance as smart or visionary.

In some cases, such as AI or space exploration, technological development is framed as a nationalistic race, though the concrete goal remains often elusive.

Questions to ask:

- Who defines this “window of opportunity,” and who benefits if others rush?
- What happens if we wait — what evidence suggests urgency is justified?
- How does the story change if the same development is treated as part of a long-term process rather than a fleeting chance?

+ Grandiose predictions
Tech reporting often amplifies claims that a new product, algorithm, or platform will transform entire industries or human life itself. They create the sense that we are standing on the brink of an inevitable transformation.

Such language compresses complexity into spectacle. It glosses over incremental progress, uncertainty, and continuity with earlier developments. Grand narratives of progress also tend to centre inventors and investors, while erasing the social, labour, and environmental contexts that make technological change possible.

Questions to ask:

- What exactly makes this technology “revolutionary”? Compared to what baseline?
- Who benefits, who bears the costs, and who is left out of the “game” being changed?
- Have similar predictions been made before? What actually happened then?

+ Valuations & investment hype
Investment announcements, funding rounds, and market projections are presented as proof of a technology’s value or inevitability. High valuations and venture capital interest become self-reinforcing signals: Media coverage attracts investors, investment attracts more coverage, and both generate public excitement that can drive further investment.

Venture capital is a high-risk investment category. Meaning, even though high funding rounds are reported as achievements, they are mostly an obligation: The money must eventually generate returns.

Furthermore, what gets funded is not always what works best, but what fits current investment narratives.

Questions to ask:

- How does the reported valuation compare to actual revenue, users, or demonstrated impact? What would need to happen for the valuation to be justified?
- Are financial projections based on proven business models, or do they assume behaviour change, regulatory approval, or technological breakthroughs that haven't yet occurred?
- How many previous “game-changing” companies in this space failed to deliver on similar promises?
```

## Best practices for reporting on topics that tend to generate hype

## ## Diversify sources

Founders, investors, and developers or researchers are often the first voices quoted in technology reporting. While they can offer insight into innovation and intent, they do not represent everyone affected by a technology. Their perspectives are typically promotional, future-oriented, and shaped by financial or reputational incentives.

Seek out the people who experience technologies in practice, not just those who design or fund them. This might include workers, end users, or communities shaped by the technology’s deployment. For instance, when reporting on a ridesharing startup, talk not only to the founders or investors but also to the drivers, local taxi unions, and passengers from different socioeconomic backgrounds.

Questions to ask:

- Does my source list overrepresent people who stand to gain from the technology’s success?
- Who is absent from this story? Whose work, labour, or lives make this technology possible but invisible?
- Who is most dependent on it — and who is most vulnerable to its failures?

## ## Go beyond first-person experiences

First-person reporting can illuminate how a technology feels to use. Still, it often reflects the experience of those technologies’ intended or ideal users. Typically, people with access, knowledge and compatibility with the product. To balance this, seek out those whose perspectives reveal the limits or exclusions of design.

Questions to ask:

- Does my personal experience reflect the average user?
- Who cannot access or afford this technology but might still be impacted?
- Who maintains or supports the infrastructure that enables my own experience?

## ## Follow the money

Journalists should look beyond the headline company or charismatic founder and examine the economic ecosystem that makes a technology viable. This includes identifying who profits at different stages, who absorbs the risks, and who provides the often-overlooked labour that sustains the system. In the case of AI, this might mean examining data labelling work, energy consumption, hardware supply chains, surveillance infrastructures, or government contracts.

Questions to ask:

- Who funds this technology, and what returns are they expecting?
- What companies, contractors, or workers are essential to making it function, but rarely mentioned?
- How does revenue actually flow through this system — subscriptions, licensing, data extraction, public procurement?
- Who bears the costs (economic, environmental, social) if the technology fails or scales unevenly?

## ## Beware the “critics” vs. “experts” narrative

In coverage, proponents are often framed as “experts” while dissenting voices are labelled as “critics,” as if they speak only from opposition rather than expertise. This framing subtly skews credibility toward those promoting novelty.

Remember that critical analysts, ethicists, labour organisers, and social scientists often have deep domain knowledge that reveals broader contexts and trade-offs. They are “experts” in the same way as proponents are, if not more so. Instead, try to broaden the vocabulary to accurately reflect actual expertise (e.g., researcher, analyst, scholar, practitioner).

Questions to ask:

- Am I presenting expertise as something only technical, or also social and ethical?
- How can I balance the narrative so that scrutiny and enthusiasm receive equal weight?

## ## Highlight uncertainties and beware of predictions

A central feature of technological hype is its tendency to smooth over uncertainties and present speculative futures as certainties. The rhetoric of inevitability (as introduced in the previous chapers) — that a technology will change everything, will replace workers, or will solve global problems — replaces complex, ongoing processes with a single linear story of progress.

Predictions in tech discourse often masquerade as evidence. Forecasts are presented in the language of probability or pseudo-science (“there’s a 70% chance of AGI by 2030,” “quantum computing will be mainstream within five years”), giving the impression that the future can be modelled and measured. In reality, these claims rest on assumptions, economic interests, and selective optimism.

In the same sense, journalists themselves should be careful with speculations in their reporting. Good reporting should instead try to excavate uncertainties and show competing perspectives. Acknowledging what we don’t know strengthens journalistic credibility and helps readers think critically about technological futures.

Questions to ask:

- Who is making the prediction, and what is their stake in its acceptance?
- What data or research supports the claim — and is it independent, peer-reviewed, or anecdotal?
- What social, political, or economic conditions would need to change for this forecast to come true?

## ## Pay attention to underhyped technologies

Hype is *selective*. It clusters around certain technologies, companies, and narratives while leaving others in the shadows — not because they are less important, but because they are less spectacular, less aligned with current investment trends, or less conducive to grand storytelling and venture capital interests (see Chapter 2).

Underhyped technologies are often those that have already proven themselves in practice but lack the narrative momentum of shinier alternatives (e.g. the disconnect in attention to solar energy vs. atomic energy). They may be open-source tools, low-tech solutions, or incremental improvements to existing systems. They might address needs in the Global South, serve marginalised communities, or prioritise maintenance and repair over replacement and growth. So are technologies developed outside dominant tech hubs, by researchers without venture backing, or in fields that lack charismatic spokespeople. The result is a distorted map of innovation that overrepresents what is loudest, not necessarily what is most useful or transformative.

By covering these technologies, journalists can offer alternative stories about what progress could also look like. This doesn't mean abandoning coverage of emerging technologies, but rather balancing it with attention to what works, what lasts, and what serves people rather than investors.

Questions to ask:

- Are there low-tech, open-source, or community-led alternatives to the high-profile solution being hyped?
- What innovations are happening outside Silicon Valley, outside English-language discourse, or outside venture-backed ecosystems?
- Who is building technology to meet present needs rather than speculative futures — and why isn't that considered newsworthy?

## ### Develop a historical awareness

Technological hype rarely appears out of nowhere. New tools are often framed as an explicit break with history, a revolution or disruption. But this is, of course, far from the truth. Technologies always have a history, and what might be presented as a sudden breakthrough is often instead just the sum of a thousand small steps over the last decades.

Past technologies — from railways and electricity to personal computers, the internet, social media, and big data — were once surrounded by confident forecasts that did not fully materialise or unfolded in unexpected ways. Revisiting earlier coverage, including your own outlet’s archives, can reveal how similar claims were framed, which actors benefited, and which risks were overlooked. History does not repeat exactly, but it often rhymes.

Questions to ask:

- Have similar claims been made about earlier technologies? What actually happened?
- How was this technology (or a comparable one) reported on five, ten, or twenty years ago?
- Whose voices were amplified or ignored in past coverage, and how did that shape public understanding?

# Conclusion

## Towards hype literacy

Hype often feels like a natural force surrounding technological development, but it is not. Hype is made. It emerges from a specific combination of bold claims, strategic communication, emotional staging, and institutional authority. When these elements circulate through newsrooms, political arenas, and investor networks, they create the impression that certain futures are not only desirable but already on their way. By recognizing hype as a constructed dynamic rather than an objective signal, journalists can begin to see where these narratives come from and whose interests they serve.

Understanding hype means paying attention to how expectations, urgency, and inevitabilities are produced. Powerful actors generate promises to attract resources, shape agendas, and position themselves as stewards of technological futures. These claims travel easily because they tap into broader cultural beliefs about innovation and progress. But beneath the surface, hype always conceals material realities: Labour, infrastructures, dependencies, and the uneven distribution of risks and benefits. When journalists learn to identify these omissions, they gain a clearer view of what is being amplified and what is being left out.

Journalists play a central role in deciding which stories gain traction, whose voices appear authoritative, and which visions of the future become publicly plausible. When reporting reproduces urgency, novelty, and speculation without scrutiny, hype gains strength and becomes harder to challenge. But when journalists slow down, diversify sources, highlight uncertainty, and frame claims as claims instead of facts, the dynamics of hype shift.

The space for critical reflection widens as journalists, as simple as it sounds, do what they are best at: Asking why. Asking why means looking behind the curtain of bold statements, questioning the speaking position of shiny personalities, unveil interests of power, speed and money, and show the consequences if attention highlights certain aspects — and overshadows others.

In the end, building hype literacy is about strengthening democratic accountability. It helps societies understand how technological futures are negotiated and whose interests dominate that negotiation. By making the mechanisms of hype visible, journalists can support more inclusive, evidence-based and plural ways of imagining the future. Hype will not disappear, but it can be contextualized, questioned, and held to account. And that makes a profound difference in how societies choose their technological paths that lead to social realities.
